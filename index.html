01 · The problem

The data exists.
The language doesn't.

How can an interpretive system help people notice meaningful patterns in their bodies — without claiming to understand their lives better than they do?

Wearables can measure the body continuously. But measurement alone does not produce understanding.

Across nearly five years of Apple Watch data, I began to see how physiological signals became more meaningful when connected to lived context — training, travel, sleep, recovery, illness. Yet the data could show that something changed without explaining why it changed, whether it mattered, or what I should do about it.

This revealed a tension between two approaches. Raw measurements leave people to construct the story themselves. Overconfident interpretations risk turning ambiguous signals into definitive explanations — and health into another optimization game.

This became the question behind Fluent: what would an interpretive layer need to do to make longitudinal health data meaningful without turning ambiguous signals into definitive explanations?

I began with a simple hypothesis: connect physiological signals to personal baseline and lived context, then help people explore the relationship between the two. I didn't yet know whether that model would hold for anyone besides me.

The goal was not to tell people how to live. It was to explore whether technology could help people see patterns, ask better questions, and understand how their current choices relate to the life they want—without pretending to understand them better than they understand themselves.

02 · What self-study revealed

Three findings.
One body can't validate them.

Nearly five years of my own data surfaced patterns I couldn't have found any other way — a fever-arc finding I had to withdraw after re-auditing it, a recovery signal hiding in a sport I'd never have guessed, and a change over nearly five years whose cause I can't separate.

Finding A

The fever arc, withdrawn

In May 2026, when I first explored my own data, an AI-assisted analysis told me my watch had detected illness 13 days before a fever: my wrist temperature and HRV had shifted together, with no symptoms. I never tested that claim. I only retraced the days around the fever and concluded I had pushed my immune system too hard. Then I published it.

I re-audited it against my own history and withdrew the finding on Oct 28, 2026. After a hot-room class and a run the next day, my wrist temperature was usually near baseline (median about +0.2°, 8 other occasions). The night after the Feb 11 run it was about +1.5° above. My HRV dip fits the first two runs over 8 miles, four days apart. My resting heart rate didn't move. I couldn't rule illness in or out.

A pattern isn't an explanation. The same signals fit more than one story, and the data alone couldn't pick between them. That changed what I think Fluent should do: not tell me what my signals mean, but show me what my history says about similar readings, ask what was going on, and let me weigh the explanations.

A second illness, in March 2025, was recorded on my older watch, which had no wrist-temperature sensor. On HRV and resting heart rate alone, it looks like the February fever: an abrupt drop and spike lasting a few days. Two episodes can't show whether that generalizes.

Finding B

The tennis discovery

Across 1,831 logged workouts spanning 10 sport types, tennis produced the strongest positive HRV signal of any activity in this dataset — HRV peaks two days after, at +4.2ms above personal baseline, still elevated a full day later. Stronger than cycling, running, HIIT, or strength training.

I had 39 tennis sessions in the dataset. I had no idea — the relationship was visible in the data, but only after I looked across the entire longitudinal record rather than at individual workouts. That changed how I thought about "understanding" health data. The system didn't give me a new measurement. It gave me a different way to ask the data a question.

Finding C

Nearly five years of change

Apple’s VO2 max estimate rose to 40.2 after I tried cross-country skiing, fell to 29.6 as I changed my training, then climbed back through half-marathon training and other activities. The estimate updates only from certain outdoor workouts, so part of this may reflect which workouts I did, not fitness alone. My resting heart rate also drifted down over the same years, as my running went from almost none to about 20–67 miles a month. The monthly series shows no step at my Nov 2025 watch switch, but a small sensor offset can’t be ruled out. I treat this as a pattern with an open cause.

These three findings shaped the questions I brought into primary research. What follows is what other people told me, including where they proved parts of this self-study wrong.

03 · The data behind it

Nearly five years of signal.
Two blood draws.
One annotation layer.

This project draws on three sources with fundamentally different temporal resolution and epistemic properties — the wearable without blood panels misses upstream causes, blood panels without the wearable miss day-to-day dynamics, and both without lived context miss the story that explains the pattern. All processing happens locally; no health data is stored externally or included in this repository.

Data processing
HealthKit XML and workout records were parsed with Python and pandas, deduplicated across overlapping sources. Function Health blood panels were extracted from PDF with pdfplumber. Fluent’s live views compare each metric against a rolling 90-day personal norm; the analyses in this case study use the mean of the previous 30 nights, and the sober experiment compares two fixed windows. Each figure names its baseline; none is a population average. That doesn't eliminate other factors, like medication or hormonal variation, that can also drive a change; it just gives a reference point for change within one person specifically.

A note on n=1
This is a single-subject longitudinal study. The findings are specific to one body, one life — not generalizable to populations. That limitation is also the point: personal health intelligence works because it's deeply individual. The design question isn't how to scale these specific findings. It's how to make this kind of individual longitudinal analysis accessible to anyone.
04 · Primary research

My data raised the questions.
Other people complicated them.

Why self-study wasn't enough

A dataset of one could only tell me what happened to me, not whether those patterns would mean anything to someone else, or whether the way I'd interpreted them would make sense to anyone else. I stopped treating my data as an answer and started treating it as a set of questions.

Who I talked to

Ten generative participants across two primary profiles — health-engaged wearable users in their 30s–40s, and adults 60+ focused on maintaining specific capabilities. Participants' health circumstances varied. Three clinical experts: an ICU nurse and two anesthesiologists. Twelve sessions total, thirteen people (one session with two participants).

Methodology
Generative interviews explored how people currently interpret their own health data and how they'd want that understanding delivered. Expert interviews tested whether the patterns emerging from those sessions held up against clinical reasoning — personal baseline computation, signal confidence, and the ethics of designing for health-anxious populations.

Methods: Generative interviews · Concept testing · Clinical expert interviews · Cross-session synthesis
From assumptions to design decisions

Research didn't just confirm or reject the assumptions Fluent started with — it revealed where the mental model behind them was too simple, and forced the product to change.

The four that shaped the design most
Overturned

Answer-first → Question-first

I thought

If Fluent interpreted the data correctly, it should say so directly.


Research showed

"If it matches what I'm feeling, I would be a little concerned. But if I'm not feeling those things, I tend to ignore it because I'm fine." When an interpretation conflicts with how someone feels, they may dismiss it.


Design implication

Observation → interpretation → verification flow, plus optional context. Interpretation stays contestable and is never presented as a verdict.

Built — see the demo below
Reframed

Data literacy → Personal relevance

I thought

Clearer insights would close the understanding gap.


Research showed

The most sophisticated participant had every tool available and still asked "why are you telling me this?"


Design implication

From explaining metrics to connecting patterns to a person's own life.

Built — see the demo below
Reframed

Annotation friction → Annotation ownership

I thought

Annotation just needed to be fast — thirty seconds, on a significant day.


Research showed

Every participant already annotates something elsewhere (cycle apps, bedtime journals, running notes). One resisted being asked to track by an app, yet journaled on their own. Speed was never the barrier; ownership was.


Design implication

Passive data comes first; annotation enriches, but never gatekeeps.


Built — see the demo below
Reframed

One voice → Felt competence by domain

I thought

People generally prefer interpretation over direct instruction.


Research showed

The same person is capable on running, lost on HRV — sometimes even overriding a signal they fully understand.


Design implication

Voice calibrates per domain — how the system infers which mode is still unsolved.

Validated framework — adaptive voice not yet built
Three mechanics-level shifts that followed from those
Overturned

Weekly delivery → Event-driven and arc-based

I thought

A weekly cadence would help build a relationship with the data.


Research showed

Zero of ten participants named weekly as a preferred cadence, in either direction.


Design implication

Delivery moved to real shifts, an annual arc, and exploring whenever curious.

Validated finding — no notification system built yet
Reframed

One long-term frame → Different future selves

I thought

Better data could connect today's choices to a life decades away.


Research showed

Seven distinct relationships to one's future self emerged — for one participant, that frame is real and frightening enough to avoid.


Design implication

Meet people inside their own time horizon — don't make them adopt Fluent's.

Reflected in persona differences — not yet its own adaptive feature
Emerged

More insight → Conservative, trustworthy silence

I thought

More useful insights would make the system more valuable.


Research showed

Trust is fragile, not additive — one mismatch against lived experience undoes several accurate calls.


Design implication

Accuracy over volume — "nothing to report" is treated as reassurance, not an empty state.

Built — see the demo below

I started with ten assumptions: one held, six were reframed, three were overturned. The one that held, naming the system's limits, confirmed by all three clinicians, grounds the verification step. The four above impacted the design most.

The assumptions that survived cleanly were borrowed directly from clinical practice — experts describing a standard they already apply elsewhere, not speculating. The ones that had to be reframed were design guesses made before anyone outside the project had been asked a single question. And the most consequential reversal — weekly cadence — was the assumption baked furthest into the build before research started: the earlier an assumption gets encoded into architecture, the more expensive it is to discover it was wrong.

05 · The demo

A system that surfaces patterns
without declaring what they mean.

The demo follows the principle that emerged from the research: start with what the system can observe, then invite the person to supply meaning. The same system is adapted to different amounts of data, life stages, and relationships to health.

The insight loop · research-informed interaction model
1

Observation

Surface a notable change or pattern.

In Fluent, Feb 11–12

On Feb 11–12, two nights in a row, your wrist temperature ran above baseline: +1.0° and +1.5°. HRV was 11–17 ms lower. Resting heart rate didn't move.

Wrist temperature: how warm your skin runs while you sleep. HRV: how much the timing between heartbeats varies. Lower HRV can be associated with strain, poor sleep, or illness.

2

Interpretation

Explain what the pattern may indicate and show the reasoning, without presenting it as a definitive verdict.

In Fluent

You did a 60-minute hot class on Feb 10 and ran 9 miles on Feb 11. After a hot class and a run the next day, your wrist temperature was usually near baseline (median about +0.2°, 8 other occasions). The night after your Feb 11 run it was about +1.5° above. The HRV dip fits your first two runs over 8 miles, four days apart.

Baseline = mean of the previous 30 nights, excluding the night scored. Exploratory, one person, data through Oct 2026 (details in the fever chart above).

3

Verification

Invite users to judge whether the interpretation fits their experience, rather than expecting them to accept it.

In Fluent

Looking back at Feb 11–12, changes like these can occur alongside feeling run down, restless sleep, or feeling unusually warm. You may not have noticed anything. How did those days feel?

I don't rememberNothing unusualRun downRestless sleepWarm at nightBody aches

The step I missed in May. The original fever-arc claim never asked whether the pattern fit how I felt.

Recall fades. Asking sooner, or with specific cues, is the next step (a design implication, not built).

4

Optional context

Leave room to connect the pattern to what was happening in their lives, whenever they choose to add it.

In Fluent

Add context in your own words or pick from the suggestions. Fluent saves it to Moments for future comparisons.

Users can add personal context at any point to inform or challenge an interpretation.

Responses in the demo are scripted; no AI model is running.

01

Start with Yvonne's own data

Fluent began as a self-study, so her nearly five years of data is the primary path — not one equally-weighted option among four fictional personas.

02

What Fluent Noticed

The system surfaces an observation first — before any interpretation is offered. The fever-arc view puts Feb 11–12 (warmer nights, lower HRV) next to Feb 24–25, the actual fever, so the comparison is visible instead of asserted.

03

Something Feels Different

Four felt-state buttons plus “I’m not sure”, each resolving through observation → interpretation → verification question → optional context — never a flat verdict. For Yvonne, each route reports what her data does show: deep sleep about 13% and REM about 7% below her 90-day average, a few single nights of lower HRV, and wrist temperature and HRV not moving together the way they did in February. It then says plainly that this is not a statement about whether she is well.

04

Moments

A personal annotation layer. Each Moment carries a topic (pattern, insight, milestone, travel, or one the person creates) and can be added with “+”. Annotating follows the same loop as the rest of Fluent: Fluent states what the data shows, offers a tentative reading, and the person checks it against how it felt, with “I don't remember” and “Nothing unusual” always available. It is timed to when annotation is already habitual, and never required for the system to keep working.

05

Jamie, Robert, and Alex

Not competing pitches — generalization tests. Does the same interpretive system hold up honestly when the amount of data, life stage, and relationship to health all change?

06

Signal framing throughout

Every comparison names its baseline: the mean of the previous 30 nights, scoring night excluded, at least 14 readings. Live snapshot cards use a labelled 90-day average. Resting heart rate and HRV are shown against the person's own baseline, and comparisons between groups of nights show how many nights are in each group, as few as three, because small groups are not findings.

07

Every chart carries a takeaway

Charts state what to notice, computed from the data, instead of describing their own shape. In Sleep, quantity is split by night of the week: Friday nights run about 45 minutes shorter than the rest (6.5h against 7.2h), a pattern the weekly average hides. Fluent cannot say why, so it does not guess. In Movement & Recovery, each sport's effect on next-day HRV is shown against the 30-night baseline, with the day of the session and the three days after on a toggle, and the number of days behind each bar. Back-to-back active days are checked against rest days, and the answer can be “no sign it costs you.” Metric names carry a ＊ that opens a plain-language note on what it measures and how far to trust it.

The current build is a pull-based exploration tool — you navigate to it, it doesn't yet reach out to you. The companion voice and event-driven delivery describe how Fluent should eventually reach someone, not how it behaves today.

All data processing happens locally. No health data is transmitted externally or included in this repository — a precondition for the kind of honest self-reflection the whole product depends on.

06 · Reflection

What Fluent
still can't know.

The cohort is entirely health-literate, employed, and already wearable-owning. The annotation-as-ownership finding is real within this group — it may not generalize to someone who's never self-tracked anything before.

The research assumes a Western biomedical framing by default. One participant, with a Chinese medicine background, surfaced a genuinely different, multi-framework relationship to health data — Fluent shouldn't present one lens as the only valid one.

The architecture assumes continuous wrist-worn capture. For at least one participant, a physical condition made that uncomfortable — they remove devices often, regardless of motivation or cost. That's a comfort-related accessibility gap, distinct from an affordability one, and nothing in the current design solves it.

The research establishes the case for an interpretation layer. It does not establish that understanding changes behavior

The open question I'm bringing into graduate research: not whether Fluent can predict something at scale, but when people trust an interpretation of their own body, when they reject one, and what actually helps them make sense of the gap between the two.
