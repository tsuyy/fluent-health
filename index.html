01 · The problem

The data exists.
The language doesn't.

How can an interpretive system help people notice meaningful patterns in their bodies — without claiming to understand their lives better than they do?

Wearables can measure the body continuously. But measurement alone does not produce understanding.

Across four years of Apple Watch data, I began to see how physiological signals became more meaningful when connected to lived context — training, travel, sleep, recovery, illness. Yet the data could show that something changed without explaining why it changed, whether it mattered, or what I should do about it.

This revealed a tension between two approaches. Raw measurements leave people to construct the story themselves. Overconfident interpretations risk turning ambiguous signals into definitive explanations — and health into another optimization game.

This became the question behind Fluent: what would an interpretive layer need to do to make longitudinal health data meaningful without turning ambiguous signals into definitive explanations?

I began with a simple hypothesis: connect physiological signals to personal baseline and lived context, then help people explore the relationship between the two. I didn't yet know whether that model would hold for anyone besides me.

The goal was not to tell people how to live. It was to explore whether technology could help people see patterns, ask better questions, and understand how their current choices relate to the life they want—without pretending to understand them better than they understand themselves.

Initial mental model: What each source can — and cannot — tell you

This was the model I started with—not a framework I assumed was correct. Research later complicated every layer.

Passive sensing
Continuous · objective · contextless

02 · What self-study revealed

Three findings.
One body can't validate them.

Four years of my own data surfaced patterns I couldn't have found any other way — a physiological pattern that became visible in retrospect before a fever, a recovery signal hiding in a sport I'd never have guessed, and a cardiovascular arc invisible at any single point in time.

Finding A

The fever arc

On February 11th — 13 days before a fever peaked at 38.2°C — HRV dropped 10ms below personal baseline and wrist temperature climbed 1.0° above baseline. Resting HR was normal. No symptoms. Neither signal alone would have been alarming. Together, they were the first readable signal of what was coming — readable only in retrospect.

By February 23rd, resting HR had spiked 33bpm above baseline and HRV had dropped 29ms below it — the clearest single day in the whole arc. In between, the numbers looked normal again for nearly a week. That recovery was real. It just wasn't the end.

HRV below baseline HRV above baseline Wrist temp elevated

The signals returned toward baseline between Feb 14 and 19. From the data alone, that looked like recovery. Six days later, a second wave appeared, and by Feb 24 the fever was impossible to miss. Read forward, each stretch was a reasonable call. Read backward, it's an obvious arc.

A second illness period in March 2025 produced a completely different temporal pattern. Two events in the same body, two different trajectories. My self-study could surface the pattern—but it couldn't tell me whether the interpretation was correct, generalizable, or useful to someone else.

Finding B

The tennis discovery

Across 1,794 logged workouts spanning 10 sport types, tennis produced the strongest positive HRV signal of any activity in this dataset — HRV peaks two days after, at +4.2ms above personal baseline, still elevated a full day later. Stronger than cycling, running, HIIT, or strength training.

I had 33 tennis sessions in the dataset. I had no idea — the relationship was visible in the data, but only after I looked across the entire longitudinal record rather than at individual workouts. That changed how I thought about "understanding" health data. The system didn't give me a new measurement. It gave me a different way to ask the data a question.

Finding C

Four years of adaptation

My trajectory wasn't linear. VO2 max rose to 40.2 after I tried cross-country skiing, fell to 29.6 as I changed my training, then climbed back through half-marathon training and other activities. The lowest point came in the middle of getting more serious about training, not before it.

These three findings shaped the questions I brought into primary research. What follows is what other people told me, including where they proved parts of this self-study wrong.

03 · The data behind it

Four years of signal.
Two blood draws.
One annotation layer.

This project draws on three sources with fundamentally different temporal resolution and epistemic properties — the wearable without blood panels misses upstream causes, blood panels without the wearable miss day-to-day dynamics, and both without lived context miss the story that explains the pattern. All processing happens locally; no health data is stored externally or included in this repository.

Data processing
HealthKit XML and workout records were parsed with Python and pandas, deduplicated across overlapping sources. Function Health blood panels were extracted from PDF with pdfplumber. Every metric is compared against a rolling 90-day personal norm — not a population average. That doesn't eliminate other factors, like medication or hormonal variation, that can also drive a change; it just gives a reference point for change within one person specifically.

A note on n=1
This is a single-subject longitudinal study. The findings are specific to one body, one life — not generalizable to populations. That limitation is also the point: personal health intelligence works because it's deeply individual. The design question isn't how to scale these specific findings. It's how to make this kind of individual longitudinal analysis accessible to anyone.
04 · Primary research

My data raised the questions.
Other people complicated them.

Why self-study wasn't enough

A dataset of one could only tell me what happened to me, not whether those patterns would mean anything to someone else, or whether the way I'd interpreted them would make sense to anyone else. I stopped treating my data as an answer and started treating it as a set of questions.

Who I talked to

Ten generative interviews across two primary profiles — health-engaged wearable users in their 30s–40s, and retired adults 60+ focused on maintaining specific capabilities — plus one in-depth interview with a participant managing a chronic condition. Three clinical experts: an ICU nurse and two anesthesiologists. Twelve sessions total.

Methodology
Generative interviews explored how people currently interpret their own health data and how they'd want that understanding delivered. Expert interviews tested whether the patterns emerging from those sessions held up against clinical reasoning — personal baseline computation, signal confidence, and the ethics of designing for health-anxious populations.
From assumptions to design decisions

Research didn't just confirm or reject the assumptions Fluent started with — it revealed where the mental model behind them was too simple, and forced the product to change.

The four that changed what Fluent fundamentally is
Overturned

Answer-first → Question-first

I thought

If Fluent interpreted the data correctly, it should say so directly.


Research showed

"If it matches what I'm feeling I'd be concerned — if not, I ignore it." People dismiss data that contradicts felt experience.


So Fluent changed

Observation, then a question, then evidence — never a verdict handed down cold.

Built — see the demo below
Reframed

Data literacy → Personal relevance

I thought

Better explanations would close the understanding gap.


Research showed

The most sophisticated participant had every tool available and still asked "why are you telling me this?"


So Fluent changed

From explaining metrics to connecting patterns to a person's own life.

Built — see the demo below
Reframed

Annotation friction → Annotation ownership

I thought

Annotation just needed to be fast — thirty seconds, on a significant day.


Research showed

Everyone already annotates something extensively, elsewhere — speed was never the barrier, ownership was.


So Fluent changed

Passive data comes first; annotation enriches, but never gatekeeps.


Built — see the demo below
Reframed

One long-term frame → Different future selves

I thought

Better data could connect today's choices to a life decades away.


Research showed

Seven distinct relationships to one's future self emerged — for one participant, that frame is real and frightening enough to avoid.


So Fluent changed

Meet people inside their own time horizon — don't make them adopt Fluent's.

Reflected in persona differences — not yet its own adaptive feature
Three mechanics-level shifts that followed from those
Overturned

Weekly delivery → Event-driven and arc-based

I thought

A weekly cadence would help build a relationship with the data.


Research showed

Zero of ten participants named weekly as a preferred cadence, in either direction.


So Fluent changed

Delivery moved to real shifts, an annual arc, and exploring whenever curious.

Validated finding — no notification system built yet
Reframed

One voice → Felt competence by domain

I thought

People generally prefer interpretation over direct instruction.


Research showed

The same person is capable on running, lost on HRV — sometimes even overriding a signal they fully understand.


So Fluent changed

Voice calibrates per domain — how the system infers which mode is still unsolved.

Validated framework — adaptive voice not yet built
Emerged

More insight → Conservative, trustworthy silence

I thought

More useful insights would make the system more valuable.


Research showed

Trust is fragile, not additive — one mismatch against lived experience undoes several accurate calls.


So Fluent changed

Accuracy over volume — "nothing to report" is treated as reassurance, not an empty state.

Built — see the demo below

The assumptions that survived cleanly were borrowed directly from clinical practice — experts describing a standard they already apply elsewhere, not speculating. The ones that had to be reframed were design guesses made before anyone outside the project had been asked a single question. And the most consequential reversal — weekly cadence — was the assumption baked furthest into the build before research started: the earlier an assumption gets encoded into architecture, the more expensive it is to discover it was wrong.

05 · The demo

A system that surfaces patterns
without declaring what they mean.

The demo follows the principle that emerged from the research: start with what the system can observe, then invite the person to supply meaning. The same system is tested across different amounts of data, life stages, and relationships to health.

01

Start with Yvonne's own data

Fluent began as a self-study, so her four years of data is the primary path — not one equally-weighted option among four fictional personas.

02

What Fluent Noticed

The system surfaces an observation first — before any interpretation is offered.

03

Something Feels Different

Five felt-state buttons, each resolving through observation → interpretation → context → a verification question — never a flat verdict. For Yvonne, all five currently resolve to a genuine, evidenced "nothing stands out," not a placeholder.

04

Moments

A personal annotation layer, timed to when annotation is already habitual — never required for the system to keep working without it.

05

Jamie, Robert, and Alex

Not competing pitches — generalization tests. Does the same interpretive system hold up honestly when the amount of data, life stage, and relationship to health all change?

06

Signal framing throughout

Resting heart rate gets a trend framing against baseline. HRV is shown as direction only, never a bare absolute number.

The current build is a pull-based exploration tool — you navigate to it, it doesn't yet reach out to you. The companion voice and event-driven delivery describe how Fluent should eventually reach someone, not how it behaves today.

All data processing happens locally. No health data is transmitted externally or included in this repository — a precondition for the kind of honest self-reflection the whole product depends on.

06 · Reflection

What Fluent
still can't know.

The cohort is entirely health-literate, employed, and already wearable-owning. The annotation-as-ownership finding is real within this group — it may not generalize to someone who's never self-tracked anything before.

The research assumes a Western biomedical framing by default. One participant, with a Chinese medicine background, surfaced a genuinely different, multi-framework relationship to health data — Fluent shouldn't present one lens as the only valid one.

The architecture assumes continuous wrist-worn capture. For at least one participant, a connective-tissue condition made that physically uncomfortable — she removes devices often, regardless of motivation or cost. That's a body-type accessibility gap, distinct from an affordability one, and nothing in the current design solves it.

The research establishes the case for an interpretation layer. It does not establish that understanding changes behavior

The open question I'm bringing into graduate research: not whether Fluent can predict something at scale, but when people trust an interpretation of their own body, when they reject one, and what actually helps them make sense of the gap between the two.
